LASER MORSE COMMUNICATOR

This project idea came to me after seeing a video online about an inteligence srategy from the 70s in which services would identify a meeting place a sensitive conversation was to take place, and listening in on this conversation by pointing a laser at the window and capturing the reflection of this laser. this meant that the sound waves casuing miniscule vibrations in the window modulates the laser reflection, and the recived laser could be reconstructed and interpreted into sound. This strategy made me realise that the same strategy could be used as a very difficult to intercept communication method. further research proved that having the laser so close to a vibrating surface causes very difficult to intepret vibrations, so instead i opted to use the laser as a binary bit. This method is interesting as interception relise on being in the direct LOS of the sender and reciever. Conversely, this is a primiative communication method BECAUSE you need LOS between sender and reciever. My design of this project uses cheap electronics to prove the theory of this method, and I aim to improve the functionality once this is proven.

Project introduction

The transmitter includes a OLED screen keyboard, 2 buttons and a dial as a tiny message typing and sending system, to be transmitted via a small laser module. Turning a dial moves the highlight, one button adds the highlighted character to the message, and the other sends it. While sending, the laser flashes each character in Morse code and the screen highlights the character on air. While morse is an outdated encryption method, this project can be easily updated to use any binary encryption to send and recieve, provided the send and recieve modules are updated with the same code.

The receiver watches a LDR for the laser, and takes an ambient lighting threshold to avoid noise. It times each flash and each gap, turns them into dots, dashes and letter breaks, and shows both the raw Morse and the decoded message on its own screen, with the option for a buzzer sounding every pulse.

Major design choices

  • Morse code. A dash is three times as long as a dot, so symbols stay easy to tell apart even with a slow light sensor and loose timing, and the two ends need no shared clock. A person can also read it by ear. This encryption method does sacrifice speed, however future versions will look to improve this.
  • A dial and two buttons to type. Three inputs cover every letter and number plus delete, keeping the transmitter small and simple to wire, at the cost of slower typing than a keypad.
  • Self-calibrating receiver. At power-up it reads the room’s light level and sets its detection threshold just above it, so it needs no manual tuning.

Design

  • Decoding by duration: the receiver timestamps every change between light and dark with millis(). A flash shorter than two dot-lengths is a dot, anything longer is a dash, and a long enough dark gap ends the letter, which is then looked up in a Morse table (unknown patterns show as “?”).
  • Live feedback while sending: the transmitter highlights the character currently on air, so the sender can follow along.
  • Editing: the last position on the dial is a delete key, so mistakes can be fixed before sending.

Results

  • 8 words per minute, by the standard “PARIS” measure: “HELLO” takes 7.8 s to send.
  • Comfortable margins on most decisions: dashes are 250 ms longer than the dot/dash threshold, and gaps sit 150 ms either side of the letter-break threshold.
  • One tight margin: the receiver is set for 100 ms dots while the transmitter sends 150 ms, so a dot is only 50 ms under the dot/dash threshold. A slow sensor stretching each flash is the most likely way for a dot to be misread as a dash.

Future developments

  • Adaptive threshold. Keep updating the ambient light level while the laser is off, instead of only at power-up.
  • Two-way link with acknowledgement. Would confirm delivery and allow resending. Not necessary for a demonstration, and it doubles the hardware.
  • Encryption methods. Further research is required to find and implement a method that sends messages quicker, or in a harder to decode method.
  • send and recieve The primary planned future development is allowing modules to operate in both send and recieve mode, this will increase hardware requirements and hence size, meaning the goal of hiding this device in an unsuspicious location or container will be difficult.
